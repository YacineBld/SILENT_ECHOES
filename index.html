THE SIGNAL

We live through screens.
What does that do to us?

Every day, humanity pours a third of its waking life into glowing rectangles. We work, learn, love, argue and grieve online. The digital world is not separate from the "real" one anymore — it shapes it, funds it, and sometimes destroys it.

This project follows the echoes our clicks leave behind: in our brains, in our classrooms, in war zones, and in the rivers that cool the machines.

CHAPTER 01 — MENTAL HEALTH

THE ATTENTION ECONOMY

"If you're not paying for the product, you are the product."

Social media is not designed to be useful — it is designed to be impossible to put down. Infinite scroll, pull-to-refresh, autoplay, streaks: every feature is a slot-machine mechanic engineered to give your brain a small dopamine reward and keep you scrolling.

The cost is measurable. Studies link heavy social-media use in teenagers to higher rates of anxiety, depression, poor sleep and loneliness. A generation that is permanently connected reports feeling more alone than any before it.

Silicon Valley insiders admitted it themselves in Netflix's documentary The Social Dilemma: teams of engineers compete to capture your attention, because your attention is what is being sold to advertisers.

"A whole generation is more anxious, more depressed… This is checkmate on humanity." — The Social Dilemma (2020)

    CHAPTER 02 — CYBERBULLYING

    SILENT ECHOES

    Behind every username, a heartbeat.

    School bullying used to end at the school gate. For today's teenagers, it follows them home, into their bedroom, into their pocket. The screen creates distance — and distance makes cruelty easier. A joke in a group chat, a "harmless" meme, a pile-on of laughing emojis: online, the audience never leaves.

    Nearly half of American teenagers have experienced online harassment. The most common forms are name-calling, spreading false rumours, and receiving unwanted explicit images. Victims report higher rates of depression, anxiety and self-harm — and most never tell an adult.

    The hardest role is not the bully's or the victim's. It is the bystander's — everyone who saw the messages and said nothing.

    CHAPTER 03 — TECH & MODERN CONFLICTS

    SILENT INTELLIGENCE

    When algorithms decide who lives.

    In April 2024, journalists at +972 Magazine and Local Call published a landmark investigation: the Israeli military was using an AI system called "Lavender" to generate kill lists in Gaza — marking around 37,000 Palestinians as suspected militants. According to six intelligence officers who spoke to the journalists, humans spent roughly 20 seconds reviewing each name before bombing — often treating the machine's output "as if it were a human decision".

    A second system, cynically nicknamed "Where's Daddy?", tracked those targets and flagged when they entered their family homes — so strikes could hit them at night, with their families inside. Officers knew the AI made mistakes in roughly 10% of cases.

    The 2024 documentary NAZA — Israeli military slang for the number of civilians permitted to be killed as "collateral damage" — puts 24 intelligence officers in front of a camera. They describe the work as "like a video game". High tech. Zero empathy.

    And it is not only bombs: surveillance systems scan faces at checkpoints, social-media posts are filtered and censored, and populations live under permanent digital observation. Technology does not just record the conflict — it industrialises it.

    CHAPTER 04 — ECOLOGY

    THE HIDDEN COST

    The cloud is not in the sky. It drinks rivers.

    "The cloud" is a marketing word for millions of servers in warehouses that run hot — and must be cooled, often with fresh water. Researchers at UC Riverside estimate that training one large AI model like GPT-3 can consume ~700,000 litres of clean water, and that a conversation of 10–50 prompts with a chatbot drinks roughly a 500ml bottle.

    Google's data centres used about 24.7 billion litres of water in 2023 — up ~17% in one year. Microsoft's consumption rose 34% from 2021 to 2022. By 2027, global AI infrastructure could demand 4–7 billion cubic metres of water a year — the annual consumption of a country like Denmark.

    The bitter irony: many data centres are built in regions already suffering drought — in Chile, Uruguay, the American West — competing with farms and families for the same water.

    CHAPTER 05 — SOLUTIONS

    RECONNECT

    Logging off is not disappearing.

    This project is not anti-technology. The same networks that enable harassment also let families stay close across oceans, let citizens document injustice, let anyone learn anything. The question is not whether we use technology — it is whether we use it, or it uses us.

    Grayscale your screen

    Colour is engineered to attract. A grey phone is a boring phone — and a quieter brain.

    Kill the slot machine

    Turn off every notification that isn't from a human you love. Pull-to-refresh is a leash.

    A digital sunset

    Screens off one hour before sleep. The algorithm will survive without you.

    Verify before you amplify

    Fake news spreads six times faster than truth. Your share button is a responsibility.

    Speak up, don't scroll past

    A single "that's not OK" in a group chat can end a pile-on. Silence is also a message.

    Count the invisible

    Attention, data, water, lives — what we can't see still costs. Keep asking who pays.
